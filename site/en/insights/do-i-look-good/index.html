Do I look good? — no tool can answer that, and here is what one can measure instead

Type that question in and dozens of tools will answer it with a number. The straighter answer is that none of them can — and the reason why is more useful than the score.

Photography3 min readBy Faceliora teamUpdated 21 September 2026

In this guide

  1. What that score out of 100 is computed from
  2. What a photograph misses that a person does not
  3. Why the question keeps coming back
  4. Questions that can be answered
  5. If you want a real answer

What that score out of 100 is computed from

Tools that score attractiveness nearly all do the same three things: find landmarks on the face in the image, compute ratios between them, then combine several of those ratios under a formula whoever wrote the program chose. Out comes a single number.

The first two steps are measurement. The third is not. Deciding which ratios matter, how much each one weighs, and which values count as good is an opinion. No experiment establishes that one set of weights is more correct than another. Somebody picked them, and the result is presented in a format that looks like a reading.

Run the same photograph through a second tool and the score changes. That is not one of them being more accurate — it is two tools reporting two different opinions.

What a photograph misses that a person does not

Even granting that such a score reflected something, it can only read one frame from one second. Most of what matters is not in it:

  • A face in motion — people see you talking, laughing, listening. What they remember is usually movement rather than proportion.
  • Grooming — hair, skin condition, posture, clothes, sleep. All changeable, and all shifting what others perceive more than bone does.
  • Context — the same face reads differently in a meeting, at a wedding, and in a profile picture.
  • Taste and culture — what counts as attractive varies by place and by decade. No formula crosses that line.

The photograph itself contributes too. Overhead light manufactures under-eye shadow that is not there. A close lens enlarges the nose. A few degrees of head rotation moves the symmetry figure. Part of any score is a score of the picture rather than the face.

Why the question keeps coming back

A score never settles it. A high one raises the suspicion that the tool was lenient; a low one sends you to the next tool. What is actually being asked for is reassurance, and a number cannot supply it.

The worse outcome is a low score from an unauditable formula feeding a decision that cannot be undone. If a number is going to carry that much weight, it is worth knowing what it measures.

Questions that can be answered

Trade "what did I score" for something a measurement can address:

  • "What about my face differs from what I assumed?" — most people misjudge their own proportions, having mostly seen themselves mirror-reversed.
  • "Has anything changed since last time?" — a value captured identically each time answers this better than anything, and it is the most useful question if you are tracking a result.
  • "Is the thing I am worried about even measurable?" — some are not, from a photograph: where the eyelid crease sits, or the true jaw angle. Knowing what cannot be measured keeps the wrong number out of the decision.
  • "If I consult a doctor, what should I ask about?" — this is where measurement earns its place. It sets an order for the conversation.
Faceliora does not issue a beauty score. The value named harmony combines several proportions under a fixed formula, and the product states plainly that it is not a rating of attractiveness — for the reasons above.

If you want a real answer

Ask someone who sees you in motion, who will be blunt, and who is not selling you anything. The answer will not be a number, and it will carry more information.

And if you are seriously considering doing something about it, start by knowing what can and cannot be measured, then take that question to a doctor — rather than taking a score.
